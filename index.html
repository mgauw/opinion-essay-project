The essay

Read it straight through, or toggle the dots above to isolate a layer.

Intro

Somewhere between a Duolingo owl and a stranger's voice on a language-exchange app, an entire classroom has quietly moved into your pocket. Technology has not merely supplemented foreign-language learning — it has rewired the very rhythm of how we acquire words, sounds, and confidence. In my opinion, modern tools offer learners advantages that traditional textbooks simply cannot replicate, though they demand discipline to use wisely.

Body I

To begin with the most tangible gain, adaptive apps turn memorisation from a chore into a habit. Algorithms such as spaced repetition resurface vocabulary precisely when it is about to slip from memory, sculpting retention rather than leaving it to chance. A student using Anki, for instance, can master a thousand words in months — a feat that once required years of dusty flashcards. Learners would therefore do well to treat such apps as a daily ritual rather than an occasional afterthought.

Body II

Beyond memorisation, though, technology dissolves geography. Video-call platforms let a teenager in Chișinău debate football with a peer in Buenos Aires, turning grammar into genuine conversation. Such authentic exchange sharpens fluency far faster than any scripted dialogue in a textbook. It is worth seeking out at least one native-speaking conversation partner online, since spontaneous dialogue exposes gaps no app can reveal.

Body III

Yet the coin has its shadow side: screens can seduce learners into shallow habits — swiping through exercises without truly internalising structure, mistaking notifications for progress. Balance, not abandonment, is the wiser recommendation.

Conclusion

Weaving these threads together, digital tools have reshaped language learning into something faster and more human. Technology deserves a central place in the classroom because it personalises and connects learning like nothing before it; however, it must remain a compass, not a crutch.

Coach's corner

What actually separates a good essay from a great one.

Structure gets you the marks for organisation. What gets you the marks for language and ideas is a bank of phrases that don't sound recycled, and arguments that feel like they were actually thought through — not assembled from a formula. Here's what I'd reach for.

Adding an idea

What's more, Layered onto this, Adding to the picture, Not only that, but

Introducing contrast

Admittedly, … still, Granted, this holds true — yet That said, Set against this,

Cause & effect

This, in turn, As a direct consequence, Which explains why The result is that

Emphasis

Crucially, Tellingly, What makes this compelling is More strikingly still,

Synthesis / conclusion

Taken together, Stitching this back to the thesis, The throughline here is What this ultimately shows is
1

Start from a scene, not a slogan. Don't open on an abstract claim — open on one concrete image (an app, a phone, a moment) and let the argument grow out of it.

2

Argue with a real skeptic in mind. Before writing a body paragraph, picture someone who disagrees. Write the sentence that would actually make them pause.

3

Make the counterargument genuinely strong. A weak "on the other hand" convinces no one — if your opposing view isn't a little uncomfortable, pick a better one.

4

Let one example do the heavy lifting. A single vivid, specific case beats three vague ones — specificity is what makes an argument feel earned rather than asserted.

5

Earn the ending. A conclusion should feel like arrival, not repetition — paraphrase the opinion, but let it carry the weight of everything the body just proved.
